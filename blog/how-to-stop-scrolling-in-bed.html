Bedtime Scrolling: Why You Can't Put Your Phone Down at Night (And How to Stop)

By Carlos Velasquez, founder of Pause · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

"Just 15 minutes, then I'm going to sleep."

If you've ever said that to yourself in bed, phone in hand, this one's for you. Because I said it pretty much every night, and it was almost never 15 minutes.

Before I had Pause, my nights went like this. I'd get in bed, open TikTok, and somehow the algorithm always knew exactly what to show me at night. For me it was music. Covers, electro beats, all kinds of music I'd find on TikTok. And here's the funny part: I'm not a musician. I don't play a single instrument. I just really enjoy music, and the feed figured that out fast haha.

So it was one video after another. And those "15 minutes" would turn into an hour and a half of me just scrolling.

The worst part wasn't even the lost time. It was what it did to me. A new video every 15 seconds, 30 seconds, a minute... it just wipes the sleepiness right out of you. I'd go in tired and come out wide awake. Not entertained, not relaxed, just hooked, going over and over.

If that sounds familiar, you're not weak and you're not broken. Bedtime scrolling is one of the most common habits out there, and there are real reasons it's so hard to stop. Let's talk about why it happens, what it actually does to your sleep, and how to stop scrolling in bed without having to throw your phone across the room.

Why bedtime scrolling is so hard to stop

Night is when the feed has the most power over you, and it's not a coincidence. A few things stack up at once.

You're at your most tired

By bedtime you've spent the whole day making decisions, holding it together at work, taking care of people. There's not much self-control left in the tank.

Researchers actually have a name for this. In 2014, a team in the Netherlands described bedtime procrastination as "failing to go to bed at the intended time" when nothing is stopping you. In their survey of 177 people, those with lower self-regulation scores procrastinated on bedtime more, and bedtime procrastination predicted not getting enough sleep (Kroese et al., 2014).

There's also a version where you stay up because it's the only time of day that feels like yours. I wrote about that one, revenge bedtime procrastination, in Why Scrolling Feels Like Rest.

There's no natural place to stop

A TV episode ends. A chapter ends. A feed never does. There's always one more video loading under your thumb, so the decision to stop is 100% on you, at the exact moment you have the least energy to make it. (More on that in Why You Can't Stop Doomscrolling.)

Short videos keep restarting the clock

This is the one that got me. Every 15 to 60 seconds you get something brand new. A new song, a new face, a new joke. Your brain never gets the boring, quiet stretch it needs to start winding down. So instead of drifting off, you get a little jolt, then another, then another.

"Just 15 minutes" has no edge

When you tell yourself 15 minutes, nothing actually happens at minute 15. No bell, no screen going dark. Your plan lives only in your head, and the app has no idea you made it.

What scrolling in bed actually does to your sleep

The short version: it takes time away from sleep, and it can make the sleep you do get start later. Here's what the research says.

Every hour in bed on a screen costs you

A big 2025 study of Norwegian university students looked at screen use after getting into bed. Each extra hour of screen time in bed was linked to 59% higher odds of insomnia symptoms and about 24 minutes less sleep (Hjetland et al., 2025).

What surprised me: social media wasn't worse than other screen activities in that study. The researchers think the main problem is simply that screen time pushes sleep time out. So it's less about which app and more about how long you stay on it. That's honestly good news, because time is something you can control.

One honest note: this was a survey taken at one point in time, so it shows a link, not proof that screens cause insomnia.

Screens can push your body clock later

In a sleep lab study, people who read on a light-emitting device before bed took longer to fall asleep, felt less sleepy in the evening, released less melatonin, had their body clock shifted later, and were less alert the next morning, compared with reading a printed book (Chang et al., 2015).

That study was about e-readers, not TikTok. But a phone glowing a few inches from your face is the same basic idea, and a feed is a lot more exciting than an e-book.

You're far from the only one

The same paper cites a survey of 1,508 American adults where 90% used some kind of electronics within an hour of bedtime at least a few nights a week. So if you scroll in bed, you're in very big company.

And it makes the next day harder

Less sleep means a more tired you tomorrow, which means less self-control tomorrow night, which makes the feed even harder to put down. It's a loop. The good part is that loops can be broken from either end.

How to stop scrolling in bed

You don't need to quit your phone or delete every app. You need an edge, something that ends the scroll for you so it doesn't all depend on tired, 11 p.m. you.

1. Give yourself a phone-free stretch before sleep

In a small randomized trial, 38 people were split into two groups, and one group was asked to stay off their phone for the 30 minutes before bed for four weeks. That group fell asleep faster, slept longer, reported better sleep quality, did better on a working memory test, and felt more positive (PLoS ONE, 2020). It was a pilot study, so it's small, but 30 minutes is a very doable place to start.

2. Make the stopping point real, not just a promise

"15 more minutes" in your head is easy to ignore. A timer, a lock, or an app that actually closes the session is much harder to ignore. The stop needs to come from outside you, because the feed won't give you one.

3. Charge your phone away from your pillow

If the phone is across the room, scrolling means getting up. That one little bit of effort is often enough to make you go "eh, never mind" and roll over.

4. Use the tools already on your iPhone

Sleep Focus and Downtime can quiet notifications and limit apps at night. They help, but the catch is that they're easy to tap past when you're half asleep. I go into why in How to Reduce Screen Time on Your iPhone.

5. Have a boring backup plan

When you put the phone down, have something low-key ready: a paper book, a podcast with the screen off, stretching, or just lying there. Boring is the point. If you need ideas, here's What to Do Instead of Doomscrolling.

6. Don't beat yourself up over one bad night

You'll slip sometimes. Everyone does. One long scroll doesn't erase your progress. Just pick it back up tomorrow night.

FAQ

Why can't I stop scrolling at night?

By bedtime you're tired and low on self-control, and a feed has no natural ending. Researchers call putting off bed when nothing is stopping you "bedtime procrastination," and it's linked to lower self-regulation and getting less sleep.

Is it bad to scroll in bed before sleep?

It can be. In one large study, every extra hour of screen time after getting into bed was linked to 59% higher odds of insomnia symptoms and about 24 minutes less sleep. A few minutes of something you enjoy isn't the problem. Losing an hour or two is.

How long before bed should I stop using my phone?

Thirty minutes is a good place to start. In a small trial, people who stayed off their phones for the 30 minutes before bed fell asleep faster and slept longer after four weeks.

Why do short videos keep me awake?

You get something brand new every few seconds, so there's never a quiet stretch for your brain to wind down. Screens before bed have also been shown to delay melatonin and push your body clock later.

How do I stop scrolling in bed without deleting my apps?

Give the scroll an ending that doesn't rely on willpower. Set a hard stop with a timer or an app like Pause, charge your phone away from your bed, and have something boring ready for when you put it down.
